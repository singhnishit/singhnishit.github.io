My motivations have been weathering a storm this past year, owing to the familiar post-teenage tension between "what I am good at" and "what I believe to be important". I began this year with a gallant quantum-computer-shaped puff in my chest, ready to work on quantum algorithms and complexity theory. I later realised that my universe was not written by [Frank Herbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Herbert), and we were getting to "intelligence" before interstellar travel. I decided to work on human-AI interactions and how they could disturb the social structures we have built. Soon after, a close friend told me about his work and urged me to publish in machine learning. A few raggedy papers and a research fellowship later, METR released findings from an investigation into "rogue" AI behaviour, prompting me to reconsider my work. I write this post after a weeklong delirium induced by rationalist thought experiments, OpenAI's Twitter handle, and papers from 2017.

*I wrote this post without LLMs; it has since had a light copyedit with their help. The expletives and perhaps unsavoury references to internet culture are still mine. I urge readers to think of these as features, not bugs* :D

## Am I a crackpot?

When you live in a country far behind the frontier (but close enough to try and emulate it), it is reasonable to be sceptical of news coming from the West. Reports of AI safety incidents landed in my inbox around the same time as they did in my peers' inboxes. We work as researchers and engineers at some of India's homegrown machine learning firms. We had never seen anything close to these problems - in fact, we're still kind of dealing with our models being dumber than us. This tension between the news we hear and the models we work with kept bringing me back to the same muddled questions: "Am I a crackpot? Am I a target for some elaborate marketing scheme? Is my internet dead[^1]? I'd really like it to be alive..."

I decided to shake these admittedly adolescent thoughts out of my head by reading things I was fairly confident humans had written (I did not want to deal with the fight-or-flight response whenever I caught an em-dash in a research paper). So I looked up papers written before LLMs were widely adopted as thought-conduits.

I don't mean to be a fear-mongering hysteric, but what I read left me uneasy. What follows is a brief writeup of the opinions, fears, and aspirations that came out of those readings.

## Roko, your basilisk is a stretch!

Soon after I came across [The rocket alignment problem](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Gg9a4y8reWKtLe3Tn/the-rocket-alignment-problem), I reached out to a friend of mine to discuss it. They brushed me off and said (verbatim) - "Bro just discovered 2018". Not very long after, I found myself reading up on the alignment canon - Roko's basilisk, Grimes, a sneaky re-read of The Last Question, and a few varieties of induction. All of these reads are admittedly more existential and "foundational" than a person like me[^2] would like. Lucky me, I also tend to love well-written stuff. I believe that much of what draws people to a subject, and keeps them there, is the chance to tell a story that hasn't been told before. I believe that Man is a Storyteller - and in the pursuit of these Stories we find seafarers and scientists, but what do I know.

Post-METR-report Nishit is a different version of pre-METR Nishit. I have been reading sci-fi for almost as long as I can remember reading, and maybe that predisposes me to stand in awe of the times we live in. I have had no hand in bringing AI to this point, but it is awesome. Over Man's introspective past, we have come up with so many egotistical ways to distinguish ourselves - cognition, intelligence, adaptation, and so on. And now we have systems checking boxes on that list. It is wonderful. It is also the first chapter of most science fiction books where things go very wrong /s[^3].

I am not very sure where this blog is headed. I expect to return to it and update it often, as my thinking develops. Hopefully a super-intelligent model doesn't kill me for anthropomorphising it in the meantime.

These days, the phrase also serves as shorthand for the feeling that much of what we encounter online is machine-generated, without necessarily implying a conspiracy.
